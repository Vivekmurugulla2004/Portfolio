Why I build

Hi, I'm Vivek.

I like figuring out how things work, whether that's software, why certain movies stick with people, or how AI changes the way people make decisions. Most of my projects start the same way: I get curious about a problem, spend way too much time understanding it, and end up building something to solve it. I'm happiest when I'm building something that didn't exist yesterday.

If there's a common thread in everything I build, it's this. I like taking things apart to understand them, then putting them back together in a way that makes more sense.

The tell

Nobody assigned me ComicArc or Reel Talk Hub. I started both because I wanted them to exist, then kept going long after "good enough" would've been a reasonable place to stop.

The obsession

I don't do casual interest. I go deep, build a spreadsheet nobody asked for, and come back a week later with strong opinions. Every project on this site is that same pattern pointed at something useful.

How I think

Technology alone isn't the product.

Every project I've shipped, a desktop app, a media business, a research assistantship, moves through the same cycle: Research → Build → Measure → Improve. I didn't set out to follow a framework. I just noticed the pattern after it worked a few times.

A feature only counts once it removes friction for a real person. I don't consider anything shipped until I can point to the problem it solved and the data that proves it.

What I care about
  • Software should respect the people using it.
  • Good design usually feels invisible.
  • Data should help answer questions, not just create dashboards.
  • The best products solve annoyances everyone else has accepted.
  • Shipping something useful beats endlessly chasing perfection.
  • Curiosity is usually a better starting point than trends.

Problem

Every comic reader I tried was cloud-dependent, hadn't been redesigned in years, or slowed to a crawl once a library grew past a few hundred issues. None of them treated a library the way a collector actually thinks about it: as something they own, not something they rent access to.

Solution

A fully native, local-first reader. It builds Publisher, Character, and Series browsing straight from how comics are already organized on disk, flags duplicates and metadata conflicts instead of silently overwriting a manual fix, and reads CBZ, CBR, and PDF through a full page or continuous-scroll reader on both platforms. The core feature is the reading-order engine: it places annuals and specials where they were actually published relative to a series, not where their filename happens to sort them, using an offline comics database that needs no live connection.

Technical highlights

100% SwiftUI on top of AppKit and UIKit, real native windows on both platforms, no web view. macOS and iPadOS share one core, the database layer, folder scanner, and reading-order engine, with SQLite accessed directly through its C API, no ORM.

The first prototype was Python and Flask behind a system web view, fast enough to validate the idea in a weekend, but a wrapped browser was never going to deliver real keyboard shortcuts or full VoiceOver, so I rewrote it natively once the concept proved out. Caching thumbnails to disk ahead of time was the difference between instant and unusable past a few thousand covers.

Impact

Problem

Reel Talk Hub started with no business plan, just a review of a movie I couldn't stop thinking about. As it grew into a real publication, good writing stopped being the bottleneck: visitors were bouncing before they finished reading, and Google Analytics showed it was a performance and discoverability problem, not a writing one.

Solution

An independently-run platform covering movies, TV, games, and comics through the same lens: does the story work, and why. Reviews, long-form criticism, a weekly newsletter, a podcast, and a presence on Letterboxd and Instagram, with editorial strategy, content planning, and every publishing decision mine to make.

Technical highlights

Vanilla HTML, CSS, and a little JavaScript, no framework, no CMS, hosted on GitHub Pages with a custom domain, which is what let me chase performance down to a perfect score instead of being capped by someone else's template. I process engagement data with Python and Excel and surface it in a Tableau dashboard that drives editorial decisions: what to cover next, what to promote, what to retire.

Impact

Diagnosed real visitor drop-off with Google Analytics and PageSpeed Insights, then fixed it directly: compressed and lazy-loaded images, cut render-blocking scripts, fixed layout shift from late-loading embeds, cleaned up heading structure and alt text.

I synthesized a corpus of 50+ peer-reviewed studies, mapping six theoretical frameworks to surface gaps in how existing research explains AI and bot-driven persuasion, turning a pile of qualitative literature into a structured gap analysis.

From there, I helped co-design a between-subjects experiment across twelve conditions to quantify how AI versus human authorship changes employee attitudes toward workplace AI adoption, contributing to experimental design, condition structuring, and variable operationalization.

I presented the literature review to Information Systems faculty in writing and in person, making the case for why established social influence theories don't fully account for non-human digital actors.

This is the same lens I bring to product work. Research isn't a phase before building. It's what tells you whether what you're building will actually land. A year of designing experiments ruined "just ship it" for me in the best way, I can't build a feature now without asking what I'm actually testing.
