Why I build

Hi, I'm Vivek.

I like figuring out how things work. Sometimes that's software. Sometimes it's why people connect with certain movies. Sometimes it's how AI changes the way people make decisions. And sometimes it's something as oddly specific as finding the best way to organize a comic collection.

Most of my projects start the same way. I get curious about a problem, spend way too much time understanding it, and eventually end up building something to solve it. That's how ComicArc started. It's how Reel Talk Hub grew from a hobby into a platform. It's even what drew me toward Human Computer Interaction research. I'm happiest when I'm building something that didn't exist yesterday.

If there's a common thread in everything I build, it's this. I like taking things apart to understand them, then putting them back together in a way that makes more sense.

The tell

ComicArc didn't exist because someone assigned it to me. Reel Talk Hub didn't exist because it was homework. Both started because I wanted them to exist, then kept going long after "good enough."

The habit

I ask a lot of questions that have nothing to do with programming, like how one changed scene in a movie would ripple through an entire trilogy, or how ComicArc would work if Apple had built it. Those aren't idle questions. They're the same instinct: pull a system apart, change one variable, see what breaks. That habit is a big part of how I design software.

The obsession

When I get into something, I really get into it. Superman, Star Wars, comic reading order, Core Web Vitals scores, whatever. I don't do casual interest. I go deep, build a spreadsheet nobody asked for, and come back a week later with strong opinions. Every project on this site is that same pattern pointed at something useful.

Why I built it

I've been collecting comics for years, and every reader I tried made me feel like a guest in my own library. So one weekend I opened a blank folder and started building the one I actually wanted. No grand business plan. I just wanted to stop being annoyed every time I opened a comic.

Who it's for

People who collect, not just people who read. If your comics folder has thousands of issues across a mix of formats and you've ever worried about losing access to your own library because a company changed its pricing, this is built for you. I built it for myself first, which is usually a decent sign it'll work for other people like me.

What was frustrating me

Most comic readers I'd used were built around a cloud subscription: your library lived on someone else's server, the interface hadn't been redesigned in years, and performance fell apart once a collection grew past a few hundred issues. None of that matched how a collector actually thinks about their library, which is as something they own.

I wanted the reader I kept wishing existed: fast, private, genuinely native, and built for large libraries from day one.

Library

Point ComicArc at the folder where your comics already live, and it builds Publisher, Character, and Series browsing straight from how you organized your files. Nothing gets renamed or moved without asking. You can add more than one library folder, a NAS share and a local drive, say, and it treats them as one combined library. Every cover shows an inline progress bar and star rating, and Continue Reading surfaces whatever you're mid-issue on.

A Possible Duplicates check flags comics that share a publisher, series, and issue number, usually the sign of a rescan or a re-download under a different filename, and a Metadata Conflicts view flags anything a rescan disagrees with instead of silently overwriting an edit you made by hand.

Reader

macOS opens the reader right inside the main window: page or continuous-scroll mode, double-page spread with automatic landscape detection, pinch and zoom, a page scrubber, autoplay, four color filters, right-to-left mode for manga, and full keyboard control. iPadOS gets its own full-screen, touch-first reader with swipe or continuous scroll, pinch to zoom, and tap zones for page turns. Both remember exactly where you left off.

Reading order, done properly

Annuals, specials, and giant-size issues almost never belong at the very end of a series, even though their filenames usually sort them there. ComicArc's reading-order engine places them where they were actually published relative to everything else, using legacy numbering, cover date, and story-arc adjacency, with a proportional fallback when nothing else is available. A free, one-time offline comics database, built from a public Grand Comics Database snapshot, handles metadata matching without ever needing a live connection, and a Fix Match picker lets you correct it by hand when it gets something wrong. Manual corrections always survive a later rescan.

Tracking, beyond a checkbox

A Diary logs every read and reread as its own dated entry, so reading history reads like an actual timeline instead of one number that keeps getting overwritten. Tier Lists let you drag comics into S through F rows. Favorite Moments saves a bookmarked page from the reader into a standalone gallery you can jump back into. Reading Paths string together an ordered, curated run across any number of series, a crossover event, a character's whole history, a best-of you're building yourself, complete with per-issue notes and a Resume button that always knows the next unfinished issue.

Stats and Year in Review

Totals for issues and pages read, time in-app, a publisher breakdown, a reading history timeline, and an annual Year in Review recap: top series, top publisher, longest streak, and your top-rated comics of the year. None of it is there to gamify reading. It's there because I was curious what my own numbers looked like.

What it actually took

There's no team here, so every decision that would normally get split across a product manager, a designer, and an engineer landed on me. Some days that meant sketching out what the library screen should feel like. Other days it meant sitting with a stack trace at midnight, or writing a README clear enough that a stranger could actually install the thing. None of it felt like separate jobs. It just felt like finishing what I started.

Architecture

ComicArc is 100% SwiftUI on top of AppKit and UIKit: real native windows and navigation on both platforms, no web view, no wrapped browser. macOS and iPadOS share one core, the database layer, the folder scanner, and the reading-order engine, and each platform builds its own interface on top of that shared core instead of one UI stretched over both. SQLite sits underneath through the raw C API directly, no ORM, no network round-trip between me and my own library. Four pieces, each doing one job:

The scanner is still the part that took the longest to get right. On macOS it watches the comics folder live through FSEvents; on iPad, which has no background file-watching, it rescans when the app comes to the foreground. Both read embedded metadata where it's available, generate and cache thumbnails, and write everything into SQLite before anything shows up in the library. CBZ and PDF are handled directly; CBR is a proprietary compression format, so it unpacks through a bundled copy of unar (the engine behind The Unarchiver) before pages ever reach the reader, macOS only, since iOS's sandbox has no shell access to run it.

The tradeoffs I made

Local-first, no accounts, ever. An account is just a place for a company to eventually charge you more or lock you out. Skipping it means the library works fully offline, nothing to sign up for, nothing to lose if a server goes down somewhere. Every "smart" cloud feature is also a new way for the app to break for reasons that have nothing to do with your internet connection. ComicArc works on a plane, and it'll still work after I've moved on to something else, since nothing about it depends on a server staying online.

Genuinely native, over a wrapped web view. My first prototype rendered the interface through a Python backend and a system web view. Fast to build, but it never quite felt like a Mac app, and there was no real path to an iPad app without starting the interface over anyway. Rebuilding in SwiftUI cost me a full rewrite, but it's the only way to get real keyboard shortcuts, full VoiceOver, native drag-and-drop, and one shared core running on two platforms instead of one.

Thumbnail caching layer. The first prototype re-rendered every cover on every scroll, which was fine for 40 comics and unusable for 4,000. Caching thumbnails ahead of time on disk is the entire difference between instant and "why is this hanging."

Sparkle over the App Store, for now. Signing, notarizing, and auto-updating outside the App Store means I can ship fixes fast while the iPad app is still catching up to macOS, at the cost of a Gatekeeper warning on first launch until notarization is fully in place.

Folder structure over manual tagging. ComicArc reads what it can from how your folders are organized and embedded metadata, and only asks you to fill in the gaps by hand. Less setup, and it respects an organization system collectors already have.

SQLite over a client-server database. Zero setup, ships as part of the app, no server process for a desktop or tablet app to babysit. It installs and just works instead of asking anyone to configure something they'll never think about again.

macOS Gatekeeper signing, imperfect as it still is. Distributing outside the App Store has to feel trustworthy or nobody installs it. Signing properly gets it most of the way there, but first launch can still trip a quarantine flag, which is why the README has a one-line terminal fix instead of pretending it never happens.

Behind the scenes

The very first version of ComicArc wasn't Swift at all. I prototyped it in Python: a Flask backend, SQLite, and a system web view wrapping the whole thing, because that stack let me go from an idea to a working screen in a single weekend. It proved the concept was worth building, and it also proved its own ceiling. A web view never quite feels native, there was no path to an iPad app without starting over anyway, and things like full VoiceOver support and real keyboard shortcuts are much harder to bolt onto a web UI after the fact than to design in from the start.

So I rebuilt it. Same product, same folder-first philosophy, entirely new foundation: SwiftUI, AppKit, and UIKit, with one shared Swift core for the database, scanner, and reading-order logic instead of two separate implementations down the line. Slower to get back to feature parity. Much stronger foundation to keep building on, and the only way to genuinely support both macOS and iPadOS from one codebase instead of maintaining two.

What I learned

That a prototype's job is to answer a question, not to become the product. Python and a web view answered "does this idea work" fast, and once the answer was yes, keeping that stack would have meant permanently capping what the app could feel like and where it could run. Knowing when a rewrite is the right call, instead of just polishing the first version indefinitely, mattered more than any single feature. The feature I'm proudest of is still the least visible one: the reading-order engine placing an annual where it was actually published instead of where its filename happens to sort it. Nobody screenshots that. It's just quietly correct every time.

What I'd redesign today

How long I waited to treat the offline comics database and the reading-order engine as one shared, platform-agnostic package from the start. Both ended up threaded through more of the codebase than they needed to be because I added the reasoning behind them incrementally. If I started the Swift rewrite over, I'd design that shared logic layer first and build the interface on top of it, instead of the other way around.

Where it's going next

iPadOS support is real today, but it still builds from Xcode rather than shipping through the App Store, since it's newer than the macOS app and I'm still hardening it against real, messy libraries the way macOS already has been. Getting ComicArcPad onto the App Store is the next real milestone, alongside finishing full notarization for macOS so the first-launch Gatekeeper warning goes away entirely.

Why not just use an existing comic reader?
I tried several. They were either cloud-dependent, hadn't been updated in years, or slowed to a crawl once my library grew past a few hundred issues. ComicArc exists because none of them solved all three problems at once.
Is ComicArc a fully native app?
Yes, 100% SwiftUI on top of AppKit and UIKit today. It started as a Python and Flask prototype behind a system web view, which was the fastest way to validate the idea, but I rebuilt it natively once the concept proved out, mainly to get real keyboard shortcuts, full VoiceOver support, and a genuine iPad app out of one shared core.
Does ComicArc ever upload my library anywhere?
No. The library database and thumbnail cache live entirely on your device. Your comic files themselves are never moved, renamed, or modified unless you use the Rename Files tool. There's no account system and no server for any of it to talk to even if it wanted to.
What platforms does it support?
macOS and iPadOS. They share one core but keep entirely separate local libraries, there's no sync between them yet. The macOS app is a signed release available now; the iPad app, ComicArcPad, currently builds from Xcode rather than shipping through the App Store, since it's newer and still catching up to macOS.
It says "ComicArc is damaged and can't be opened." Is it actually broken?
No, that's macOS quarantining an app that isn't notarized yet, not real damage. Run xattr -cr /Applications/ComicArc.app in Terminal once and relaunch. I'd rather document the fix honestly than pretend it doesn't happen.
Is it really free?
Yes. It's open source under the MIT license. No premium tier, no paywall planned.
Where do I actually get comic files?
ComicArc is a reader, not a store. I point people toward DriveThruComics and Humble Bundle for DRM-free CBZ and PDF, Kindle or ComiXology if you'd rather buy through Amazon, Hoopla or Libby if your library card covers digital comics, and the Internet Archive for public domain work. Only import what you actually own or have the right to use.
Can I contribute or report a bug?
Yes, the repository is open on GitHub with issues enabled. That's also the fastest way to see what's actively being worked on.

Why I built it

I like talking about movies more than is probably normal. Reel Talk Hub started as a place to put that energy somewhere other than group chats where nobody asked. There was no business plan on day one, just a domain name and a review of a movie I couldn't stop thinking about.

The mission statement on the site hasn't really changed since: "I care deeply about storytelling, whether it comes from a big studio release, a cult favorite, or something that makes you stop and think." Everything else, the games coverage, the comics, the actual website, came later. That sentence came first.

Who it's for

People who want an actual point of view on the things they watch, play, and read, not an algorithm-optimized summary. People who read a review because they want to argue with it, not just check a star rating. That used to mean film fans specifically. Now it means film, TV, game, and comic fans, since the site grew to cover all four with the same lens.

What was frustrating me

I was writing things I was proud of and watching visitors bounce before they finished reading. It stopped feeling like a writing problem once I actually opened Google Analytics. It was a performance and discoverability problem, and I'd been ignoring it.

What it actually takes

On a Tuesday I might be drafting a review, on a Wednesday I'm in Google Analytics trying to figure out why a page tanked, and on a Thursday I'm knee-deep in CSS because something looked wrong on mobile. Somewhere along the way this became a real platform: reviews and long-form criticism across four mediums, a weekly newsletter, a podcast, and a presence on Letterboxd and Instagram, with editorial strategy, content planning, and publishing decisions all mine to make. Nobody hands me a brief. I write it for myself and then have to live with it.

Behind the scenes

Reel Talk Hub didn't start as a website. In 2023 it was closer to a personal writing exercise, reviews I wanted to write whether or not anyone read them. By 2024 the scope had grown past movies into games and comics, because I was applying the same "why does this story work" question to whatever I was actually consuming that month, and artificially keeping it film-only stopped making sense.

The site itself, the one live today, is the newest part of the project. I built it independently in 2025 as a permanent archive for everything that had been scattered across earlier writing, rather than another platform I didn't fully control. That's also the point where "run a media platform" stopped being a metaphor and started meaning actual engineering: performance, SEO, and analytics work, not just writing.

Diagnosis, fix, result

Visitor drop-off was high, so I treated it as a measurement problem before a design problem. Google Analytics and PageSpeed Insights pointed to specific performance gaps, and I made targeted engineering changes across page speed, search discoverability, and accessibility. The result was a perfect Core Web Vitals score.

100 / 100 Core Web Vitals

Reached by diagnosing real user drop-off with analytics tooling, then engineering fixes across load performance, layout stability, and accessibility, not by guessing what "felt slow."

The specific fixes: compressing and lazy-loading images that were the biggest contributor to load time, cutting render-blocking scripts, fixing layout shift caused by ads and embeds loading late, and cleaning up heading structure and alt text that search engines and screen readers both depend on. None of it was glamorous. All of it moved the number.

The analytics loop

If I can measure it, I can probably improve it. That's basically the operating principle behind Reel Talk Hub's whole analytics setup. I process and clean audience engagement data with Python and Excel, then surface it in an interactive Tableau dashboard tracking content performance and viewer behavior. Editorial and publishing decisions, like what to cover next, what to promote, what to retire, come from that dashboard, not from instinct. That said, the dashboard tells me what's working, it doesn't tell me what to write next. I still pick the movie because I have something to say about it; the data just tells me whether I said it in a way anyone could find. Data doesn't replace taste, it just tells you if your taste is reaching anyone.

The site also carries affiliate links to the streaming platforms a review talks about, Netflix, Prime Video, Disney+, Hulu, Max, Apple TV+. It's not a real revenue line, but it's a real one, and it's the same lesson as the analytics: a media business, even a tiny one, has to think about how it actually makes money, not just how many people show up.

What I learned

The bigger lesson was that good writing and a discoverable site are two different jobs. I used to think finishing the article was the finish line. Now I know publishing it is closer to the starting line. A great review nobody finds might as well not exist.

What I'd redesign today

The publishing workflow. I still write, format, and check performance in three separate places out of habit, because that's the order I built things in, not because it's the order that makes sense. A cleaner setup would fold the SEO and speed checks into the same step as writing, so I catch problems before I hit publish instead of after Google Analytics tells me.

Where it's going next

The newsletter and the Letterboxd and Instagram presence solved discovery. What's next is depth: a closer look at what the Tableau dashboard is actually telling me about repeat readers versus one-time visitors, and more video content now that the writing side of all four mediums is in a steady rhythm.
